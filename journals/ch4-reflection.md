# Chapter 4 Self Reflection — **Knowing What to Do: Constraints, Discoverability, and Feedback**

**Key point:** Good design helps people understand what to do by using knowledge from the world instead of forcing them to figure things out through trial and error.

Chapter 4 explains how designers can guide people through unfamiliar objects and situations by focusing on physical, cultural, semantic, and logical constraints, along with signifiers, mappings, forcing functions, conventions, and feedback. Norman shows that a design can be technically functional and still be difficult to use when it does not communicate its possible actions clearly.

#### Four Kinds of Constraints

Norman begins with the example of building a Lego motorcycle. The fifteen pieces do not need detailed instructions because several kinds of constraints help determine where each piece belongs. Physical constraints limit which pieces can fit together. Cultural constraints help people understand the meaning of colors and familiar objects. Semantic constraints use the meaning of the situation, such as knowing that a rider should face forward. Logical constraints help when only one possible piece remains.

Norman summarizes the value of constraints this way:
> “Constraints are powerful clues, limiting the set of possible actions. The thoughtful use of constraints in design lets people readily determine the proper course of action, even in a novel situation.” (Norman, 2013, p. 125)

This idea stood out to me because good design prevents mistakes instead of making users guess. The battery example shows how shape, position, and connection guides can make errors difficult or impossible without requiring users to read a manual or conduct research.

#### Doors, Switches, and Natural Mapping

Norman uses doors to show the importance of discoverability. A flat plate suggests pushing, while a handle suggests pulling. If a door needs a sign explaining how to open it, the design has failed:

> “Somehow, when a device as simple as a door has to have a sign telling you whether to pull, push, or slide, then it is a failure, poorly designed.” (Norman, 2013, p. 133)

The same problem appears with identical light switches. Norman’s Figure 4.5 improves this design by arranging the switches like the lights they control, creating a natural mapping. He also explains that poor communication during the design process often causes confusing systems:

> “The lack of clear communication among the people and organizations constructing parts of a system is perhaps the most common cause of complicated, confusing designs.” (Norman, 2013, p. 137)

This connects to my interest in software design because a system can work technically while still confusing users. Designers must understand the user’s task before deciding how controls should be placed or labeled.

#### Forcing Functions and Safety

Another important idea is the forcing function, which uses constraints to prevent unsafe actions until required steps are completed. Norman describes interlocks, lock-ins, and lockouts as examples. These features guide users through the correct sequence, keep important actions active, or prevent dangerous actions. They reduce the need to remember every rule, especially during stressful situations. However, forcing functions should not be so inconvenient that users disable or work around them.

#### Conventions, Change, and Standardization

Conventions help people understand new designs by connecting them to what they already know, but new conventions can feel confusing. Destination-control elevators are more efficient, yet they violate the expectation of choosing a floor inside the elevator. 

Norman makes an important distinction:
> “Just because something is different does not mean it is bad.” (Norman, 2013, p. 149)

Designers should introduce changes carefully because users need time to develop a new mental model.

The faucet example shows why standardization matters. Controls should match users’ expectations about temperature and water flow. Norman summarizes this principle simply:

> “If all else fails, standardize.” (Norman, 2013, p. 155)

Consistent standards reduce learning and help users transfer knowledge between products.

#### A New Perspective

Chapter 4 reinforced that discoverability is a major part of usability. A user should not have to guess what a control does, remember an unusual mapping, or search for instructions. Constraints can reduce wrong choices, natural mappings can make relationships clear, forcing functions can prevent dangerous sequences, conventions can transfer previous knowledge, and feedback can confirm what happened.

Moving forward, I will evaluate a design by asking whether the next action is discoverable, whether the controls match the user’s mental model, and whether the system gives clear feedback after an action. A design should not only make an operation possible; it should also help people know what to do and understand what happened.
