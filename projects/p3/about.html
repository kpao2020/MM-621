Behind the project · Ken Pao · November 2026

Designing for a
small daily decision.

Forecast Meal is an MM-621 experiment in making the path from “What should I cook?” to a usable idea feel clear and inviting.

The project

From data to dinner.

The experience pairs today’s forecast with a person’s food preferences to suggest a single recipe.

My initial Basket Buddy concept explored local grocery prices, but research showed that many reported prices were old and ingredient matching could be impractical. Feedback about convenience, cuisine, and substitutions helped me shift toward a more focused cooking interaction.

Forecast Meal is a single-purpose tool that helps people make a meal decision with minimal effort. Forecast Meal carries forward the weather work from Project 2 - weather data visualization, the unexplored forecast data integrated into AI-genereated recipes, and the design principles of clarity, discoverability, and feedback.

Its mechanics follow a sequence: choose a location, review the forecast, set preferences, request a recipe, and adjust or substitute ingredients. The visual language uses warm cream, greens, rounded cards, weather imagery, and small nutrition icons to make the experience feel calm and approachable.

Design reflection

Learning from everyday things.

Donald Norman’s The Design of Everyday Things and my journal reflections shaped how I think about the relationship between the interface and the person using it.

01

Mapping & hierarchy

The numbered sections follow the task: location, forecast, then meal preferences. Controls sit near the information they affect, helping people understand the relationship between their choices and the result.

02

Signifiers & discoverability

Labels, familiar form controls, action buttons, and expandable forecast details indicate where to act and what to expect. The goal is to make the next step visible without relying on memory.

03

Feedback

Loading and status messages, the displayed location, forecast details, and recipe result help people see what the system is doing and evaluate the outcome. Clear feedback also matters when a request cannot be completed.

04

Constraints & choice

Defined options and a one-to-four serving range reduce unsupported inputs, while an optional location choice and ingredient substitutions leave room for personal needs. Preferences still require the user’s judgment.

Seven stages of action

A journey from intention to evaluation

A person forms a goal to find a meal, plans to use the forecast, and specifies a location and preferences. They perform the request, perceive the returned recipe, interpret its ingredients and nutrition, and compare it with what they wanted. Adjustments and substitutions support another pass through that cycle.

This is a design intention to evaluate with users: the presence of a control alone does not prove that its meaning or feedback is clear.
